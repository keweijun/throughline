# Lessons are taught in steps with check questions

A section's first lesson used to be one long reply followed by grouped practice. In a real course (a physical chemistry course, 13 sections) these replies ran 3,000–6,000 characters and covered up to ten subtopics. Most of the learner's side questions were "what is X?" about a term or step the lesson had skipped, and nothing in the lesson noticed the gap before the next idea built on it. The same course's first lessons, taught in Codex chat in steps of about 1,500 characters that each ended with a short question, had none of these leaps.

We teach a section's lesson as a short sequence of **lesson steps**:

- The teacher plans the steps in its first reply, from the section's core question, its overview and the preparation material; the overview's `steps` are only a fallback when that reply plans none. In a trial on a real course, a teacher handed the overview's steps as the plan kept them unchanged, while one asked to plan grouped the material better and reused its example across steps. The teacher decides how many steps the section needs; the host only caps the plan at a sanity limit. No count is written into the prompts. Instead a step is sized by what the learner can absorb before going on, and a new step starts where the source pages reach a result or derivation the next part builds on, so a section presenting one idea is one step and one presenting several results with a proof is not. Without that guidance the count settled on a habit instead of the content: teachers first gave most sections three steps, and once told a section may be one step, they taught seven-page sections with a full proof as one step, shortening the lecture's derivation to fit. The learner then asked where lines of the proof had gone. A step therefore keeps every line of a derivation the lecture shows; a derivation that does not fit beside the rest of a step gets its own step. A step is one turn of the mainline lesson and keeps the lesson lifecycle: it streams, can be paused, and is regenerated as a new attempt.
- A step may end with one **check question**, when the teacher judges that a quick question would catch a likely gap at that point: multiple choice, with options written around likely misconceptions and an "I'm not sure" option. The step's turn returns the question with the correct option and a one-line explanation for every option, so the learner sees feedback at once without another model turn.
- A correct answer starts the next step at once, without Continue (usually already written while the learner read). A wrong or "not sure" answer shows that option's explanation and the correct answer with its own, and the next step opens with a short fix aimed at that misconception before going on. Repairs never cost an extra turn.
- The learner may continue without answering. The check is then left unanswered and nothing is repaired.
- The teacher may adapt the plan freely while teaching: add a bridge step, reorder or merge the remaining steps. It may only use material that belongs to the section or its taught prerequisites; content of later sections stays with them (ADR 0006). The interface shows the step position and any added step.
- Grouped practice still ends the section. Check questions are not exercises. Lessons follow the lecture: the section's source pages, in their order and depth, with their own worked examples; corrections added while building a course are applied in a sentence where they change a statement, and plan topics the pages do not present get at most a remark (a trial lesson that taught every build-time caveat as a topic felt far from the notes and too hard). Lessons teach a method on examples other than the listed exercises and leave the section's original exercises (homework, problem sets) unsolved for practice: in a trial, a lesson that worked the homework as examples left practice nothing original to set.

## Check questions are not evidence

A check answer is recorded as an interaction, never through `evidence.assess`, and does not change a section's practice or evidence status. Multiple choice with the answer generated in advance cannot show independent work. Repeated misses on one idea become a difficulty in course memory (ADR 0010), which is how they reach later teaching. Checks are also not retrieval practice: they do not make a section reviewable or count as a review (ADR 0008), which still comes from assessed exercises.

## Consequences

- A lesson takes several turns; preparation may draft the first step ahead. In trials a step took 30–70 s to write, all of it spent after the learner pressed Continue, so the next step is now written as soon as the current one completes, hidden as a **prepared step** until Continue reveals it, still streaming or already complete. Only the check answer changes what the next step says, so the prepared step's prompt records the repair it assumed (none while the check is unanswered): a correct answer, or none, keeps it; a wrong or "not sure" answer replaces it with one that opens with the repair, still written while the learner reads the feedback. A prepared step is not an interaction, evidence or a taught step until revealed; pausing the lesson, starting another lesson or leaving the section drops it, and a lesson has at most one. It costs a model turn whenever it is dropped, which we accept for the wait it saves. Preparation of the next section still waits while a step is being written.
- Lessons saved before this change stay single replies and are shown as they are.
